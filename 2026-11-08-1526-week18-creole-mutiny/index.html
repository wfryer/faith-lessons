Overview

Centering text: Acts 16:25 to 34

At midnight, Paul and Silas are singing in prison when an earthquake opens every door and unfastens every chain. The jailer, who expected to find an empty prison, finds his whole household changed. Read it in the NRSVUE.

On a slave ship off the Bahamas, nineteen men did not wait for an earthquake. Four ideas to carry through the lesson:

A story that ends in NassauProject 1526 reminds us that we tend to tell resistance stories only when they end in martyrdom. This one ends in a harbor with 128 people walking free.
Many hands opened the doorNineteen men seized the ship. Black Bahamians, British Army soldiers and British law shaped what happened next. Historian Jeffrey Kerr-Ritchie calls it self-liberation.
Freedom was not wholeFive people were still aboard when the Creole reached New Orleans, and they were sold. Two of the nineteen died in custody. Honest telling holds the 128 and the five together.
The books were kept for the enslaversBritain set aside £20 million for enslavers in 1833. A commission paid claimants for the Creole's captives in 1855. The people freed received nothing from either.

The story in six scenes

  1. 1839 to 1841

    A man who went back

    Madison Washington escapes to Canada, then returns to Virginia for his wife. He is recaptured and sold in Richmond to a slave trader bound for New Orleans.

  2. Oct 25, 1841

    A ship in the coastwise trade

    The brig Creole leaves Richmond with about 135 enslaved people, a crew of ten and white passengers, including slave traders. The ban on importing people from Africa does not touch this trade.

  3. Sun, Nov 7, about 9:30 PM

    The rising

    Gifford finds Washington in the women's hold. Washington shoves him down and calls to the men below: "Come on, my boys, we have commenced and must go through." Nineteen men take the deck. By about 1 AM the ship is secured.

  4. Nov 7 to 9

    A destination

    Liberia is ruled out for lack of food and water. Ben Blacksmith argues for the British West Indies, where the Hermosa captives had been freed. Overseer William Merritt is made to navigate to Nassau.

  5. Nov 9 to 13

    Nassau harbor

    Black Bahamian pilots tell the captives they are free. Soldiers guard the ship, a crowd gathers in small boats, and a US attempt to retake the brig fails. On Nov 13 the Attorney General holds the nineteen men and tells everyone else they may go ashore.

  6. Dec 1841 to 1855

    After Nassau

    The Creole reaches New Orleans with five people still aboard. In April 1842 the 17 surviving men are released. The two governments argue over property until a commission pays the claimants in 1855.

Counts vary by source. Between 134 and 139 enslaved people were aboard, and 128 gained their freedom. Dates and details below are checked against the sources listed at the bottom of this page, and each timeline entry names its sources.

People

Madison Washington

Born into slavery in Virginia. Escaped to Canada, then went back south for his wife and was recaptured and sold. Aboard the Creole he worked as head cook for the enslaved, which gave him freedom to move and to watch the crew. Named by officers and crew as a leader of the rising. Released from Nassau in April 1842; his later life is not documented in the sources checked here.

Sources: HISTORY.com; Wikipedia

Ben Blacksmith (also Ben Johnstone or Johnson)

One of four leaders named in the officers' depositions. HISTORY.com says he led three others who killed John Hewell and wounded the captain, and that he held a musket on the first mate. Wikipedia credits him with proposing the British West Indies, because he knew the Hermosa captives had been freed there.

Sources: HISTORY.com; Wikipedia

Elijah Morris

A leader of the rising. Kerr-Ritchie found that Morris became a landowner, farmer and fisherman in the village of Gambier in the Bahamas, and that his son Alexander inherited his property.

Sources: Kerr-Ritchie; 64 Parishes

Doc (Doctor) Ruffin

The fourth leader named in the depositions. The sources checked here give little else about him.

Sources: Wikipedia; Nevius review

Susan

Washington's wife, named in later accounts including Chadwick's. Why he returned south is the heart of the story. Whether she was aboard the Creole is disputed, and nothing in the sources checked says what became of her.

Sources: BlackPast; World History; Wikipedia

The five who stayed aboard

Three women, a girl and a boy were aboard when the Creole reached New Orleans on Dec 2, 1841. Accounts differ on whether they chose to stay or were hiding. They were sold into slavery. No names appear in the sources checked.

Sources: Wikipedia; BlackPast; National Archives

Black Bahamians and the 2nd West India Regiment

The harbor pilot and his crew told the captives they were free. A crowd in small boats surrounded the ship. Free Black soldiers of the British Army's 2nd West India Regiment guarded the ship and the captives. Without them the outcome could have been different.

Sources: Wikipedia; HISTORY.com; Nevius review

Why 1833 is the hinge. Britain abolished slavery in its colonies, the Bahamas included, with full legal emancipation by 1838. Eight years after the Act, the Creole sailed into Nassau Harbor. A sea route that was legal for American slave traders ended in a British colony where slavery had been abolished. That collision of two legal systems is why 128 people walked free.

Events

Numbered in order. Use the buttons to follow one thread: the long arc, the man, the voyage, the harbor, the law, the politics, the earlier ships, or memory and money.

  1. 01

    1807 to 1808Key moment

    The United States bans the transatlantic slave trade (passed in 1807, in force in 1808; sources give either year), and Britain bans its own slave trade in 1807. Neither ban ends slavery. Slavery stays legal in the United States, and shipping enslaved people by sea from Chesapeake ports to New Orleans, the coastwise trade, stays legal too. The Creole's voyage in 1841 follows exactly this legal route.

    Arc of historyLaw and diplomacy

    Sources: Wikipedia; HISTORY.com

  2. 02

    Late 1830

    The American brig Comet, carrying enslaved people in the coastwise trade, wrecks off Abaco in the Bahamas. Officials in Nassau free about 165 people (sources give 164 or 165). It is the first of several such cases.

    Earlier ships

    Sources: Wikipedia: Enterprise

  3. 03

    Aug 1833Key moment

    Parliament passes the Slavery Abolition Act (royal assent Aug 28, 1833). Freedom takes effect Aug 1, 1834, followed by a forced "apprenticeship" system, and full legal emancipation arrives by 1838. Parliament sets aside £20 million to compensate enslavers. The people who had been enslaved receive no compensation. This is the legal hinge of the Creole story: by November 1841 the Bahamas is a British colony where slavery has been abolished, only a short sail from US waters where it is still legal.

    Law and diplomacyArc of history

    Sources: Full Fact; Historic UK

  4. 04

    Feb 1834

    The Encomium, another coastwise ship, wrecks off Abaco. About 45 people are freed. (Some sources date this case to 1833.)

    Earlier ships

    Sources: Wikipedia: Enterprise; Wikipedia

  5. 05

    Feb 1835

    A hurricane drives the brig Enterprise into Bermuda with 78 enslaved people aboard. Bermuda's court frees them. Seventy-two choose to stay; one woman and her five children choose to return to the United States.

    1839

    The Amistad rebellion. Fifty-three captives, held in violation of the transatlantic ban, seize their ship.     1839 or 1840

    Madison Washington escapes slavery in Virginia and reaches Canada. Sources give 1839 or 1840. He travels through the Underground Railroad network and meets abolitionists, including Robert Purvis in Philadelphia.

    1840

    The schooner Hermosa, carrying 38 people from Richmond toward New Orleans, runs aground in the Bahamas. British magistrates free them over American protests. A year later a leader of the Creole rising, Ben Blacksmith, cites this case.

    1841, before October

    Washington returns south to Virginia to find his wife, Susan. He is recaptured and sold in Richmond to a slave trader. Sources disagree on whether his wife was among those shipped on the Creole.

    Mon, Oct 25, 1841

    The brig Creole leaves Richmond just after midnight, bound for the New Orleans slave market. Another group is taken on at Hampton Roads (Wikipedia counts 103 from Richmond and 32 added there).     Sun, Nov 7, 1841, about 9:30 PMKey moment

Going deeper

Eight threads worth pulling after the lecture and the slides.

The 128 and the five

The headline number is 128, not 135. Five people, three women and two children, were aboard when the ship reached New Orleans. They were sold. A story that wins still has people it did not reach, and the women's hold is where Washington was found when the rising began. Where are the women in this story?

Many hands opened the door

The harbor pilot and crew, the crowd in small boats and the soldiers of the 2nd West India Regiment were Black people in Nassau acting in solidarity. Jeffrey Kerr-Ritchie argues the captives' freedom was self-liberation, not a gift of British benevolence. British officials, meanwhile, still held the nineteen men for months.

Information traveled

Earlier ships (Comet, Encomium, Enterprise, Hermosa) had been freed in British ports, and enslaved people on the Creole had heard of the Hermosa. Washington's route through Philadelphia put him in the home of Robert Purvis, who owned a portrait of Cinque. The Amistad was not the Creole's rival for attention but its inspiration.

Who owns people at sea?

Daniel Webster said the captives were property under the Law of Nations. Britain said slavery had no standing in its law. Joshua Giddings said slavery exists only where local law creates it, and was censured for saying so. The Webster-Ashburton Treaty left the question open and left slave revolts off its extradition list.

Whose telling is this?

Much of what we know comes from depositions given by the ship's officers and crew, newspaper reports, the abolitionist press and Douglass's fiction. The detail that Washington stopped the killing and bandaged the sailors, for example, is reported by others. Kerr-Ritchie notes that an 1842 Liberator article made Washington the face of the revolt. No account in the sources checked here comes from the nineteen men themselves.

What became of them

Elijah Morris became a landowner in Gambier, Bahamas, and his son inherited his property. For Washington, Doc Ruffin, Ben Blacksmith and the others, the sources checked here are mostly silent. Several people on the ship went to Jamaica and others stayed in Nassau.

Two governments, one ledger

In 1833 Britain set aside £20 million to compensate enslavers and nothing for the people freed. In 1855 a commission paid the claimants for the Creole's captives. Both governments paid owners. Project 1526 calls this the empire as bookkeeper.

A closer look at Acts 16

Our reading starts at verse 25, with the earthquake and the opened doors. The verses just before it set the scene: an enslaved girl whose owners make money from her, and owners who drag Paul and Silas to court when the profit is gone (Acts 16:16 to 24). The money in both stories belongs to the owners.

Discussion questions

  1. UnderstandingWho made the difference once the Creole reached Nassau: the nineteen men, Black Bahamians, British soldiers, British law, or all of them? What does the usual telling emphasize, and what does it leave out?
  2. FaithIn Acts 16 the doors open and the chains fall, and the prisoners stay. On the Creole the people on the ship sail. What do we mean when we say the gospel of release works through people who take risks?
  3. PersonalMadison Washington reached Canada and went back for his wife. What would you go back for? Who has taken that kind of risk for you?
  4. Our group and our congregationFive people were still aboard when the ship reached New Orleans, and most of what we know about the nineteen comes from the men who held them captive. How do we tell a story that wins without forgetting the people it did not reach? What stories like this one are missing from what our own church teaches?
  5. AccountabilityIn both 1833 and 1855 governments paid enslavers and not the people freed. What does that tell us about whose losses count? What do we think a just accounting looks like today?
